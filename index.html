Worn in memory of the land before us

The collectionVagari Vati, by Jessian Titus

This collection is Jessian Titus’s tribute to his ancestors and to their land in Africa.

It is cut loose, for a city a long way from that land. What carries the memory across is the print.

The people of the river

OriginBamana, middle Niger, MaliDraft for Jessian to confirm

The marks on these prints point to one people more than any other: the Bamana, who live along the middle Niger in what is now Mali.

The Bamana are a Mande people. They farmed millet and sorghum on the river’s floodplain, and in the eighteenth century they built a kingdom with its capital at Ségou, on the riverbank. Their language, Bamanankan, is still the one most people in Mali speak.

At planting time they danced Ci Wara, carved antelope headdresses that honour the first farmer, the one who taught people to work the land. Respect for the land, and for the people who worked it first, runs through everything they made.

The Bamana are best known far from home for one thing: a cloth painted with mud.

Cloth made with earth

BògòlanfiniBògò, earth. Lan, by means of. Fini, cloth.
  1. 01

    Weave

    Men weave cotton into narrow strips on a portable loom, then sew the strips edge to edge into one cloth.

  2. 02

    Dye

    The cloth soaks in a bath of crushed n’gallama leaves and dries in the sun a soft yellow.

  3. 03

    Paint

    Women paint the marks with mud from the riverbed, left to ferment in a clay jar, sometimes for a year.

  4. 04

    Fix

    Iron in the mud meets the tannin from the leaves. Where the mud lay, the cloth turns black.

  5. 05

    Repeat

    The cloth is washed, painted and dried again, often many times, until the black is deep and the marks are clean.

A cloth that protects

MeaningHunters, mothers, memory

Mud cloth was never only decoration.

Hunters, the donso, wore it into the bush, where its dark ground hid them and its marks were said to guard them. Young women were wrapped in it after initiation, and mothers after giving birth, because the cloth was believed to take in harm.

Every pattern had a name. Many recalled a proverb, a battle, a person or a place, so a finished cloth could be read by anyone who knew the marks. It was a record kept in earth, on cotton, by hand.

Eleven prints. Each one says something.

The patternsSorted into four families

In much of West Africa, cloth is a place to keep memory. On Malian mud cloth, bògòlanfini, each mark is painted by hand in fermented mud and stands for something its maker knows: a place, a saying, a passage in a life.

Most of the prints here are made of the same kinds of shapes. A few, the florals and the paisley, came by other roads, and we say so where they do.

From the Joliba to New York

Vagari VatiBy Jessian Titus

Jessian grew up a long way from the river. Vagari Vati is how he goes back.

The old marks are redrawn and printed on loose kimonos, long dresses, wrap pants and soft tees, cut for city streets rather than the field. They are made in small batches and shipped worldwide from India.

Mud cloth was always read as well as worn. These pieces ask the same of the people who wear them: know what the marks say, and carry them on.

Words to know

Bamana
A Mande people of the middle Niger in Mali, also called Bambara.
Joliba
The Mande name for the Niger, “the great river”.
Bògòlanfini
Mud cloth: cotton dyed with leaves and painted with fermented river mud.
N’gallama
The tree whose leaves give the yellow dye that turns the mud black.
Donso
The hunters’ brotherhood, who wore mud cloth into the bush.
Ci Wara
The antelope headdress danced at planting, honouring the first farmer.

A record you can wear

Ancestry

A pattern like these is repeated by hand, row after row, by someone who learned it from someone else. Wearing it keeps that chain going one more link.

Each piece is named for an idea worth carrying, and printed with the marks of the people who carried it first.
